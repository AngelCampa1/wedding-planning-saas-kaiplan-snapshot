## How Long Do You Actually Have

Three months. That is the standard window, and it has been for a long time. The "two weeks" rule you may have heard is not real — it causes couples to panic mid-honeymoon about thank you notes instead of enjoying their trip.

What is real: notes that arrive four, five, or six months after the wedding start to feel like an afterthought. The three-month window gives you breathing room without sliding into that territory.

Start writing before your honeymoon if you can. Open gifts as they arrive during the engagement and write notes immediately. By the time the wedding itself happens, you may have already sent 30-40% of your notes.

## Who Gets a Note and Who Doesn't

Everyone who gave a gift gets a written thank you note. No exceptions, regardless of how well you know them or how small the gift.

People who attended but did not give a gift do not get a formal note. You already thanked them in person at the wedding.

Your vendors do not get personal thank you notes (though a review on Google or The Knot is worth more to them). Your officiant is an exception — a short personal note is a nice touch.

Wedding party members deserve acknowledgment, but this typically happens through the gift you give them. A separate card is a thoughtful extra, not required.

Parents and close family who contributed significantly to the wedding financially or logistically deserve a longer, more personal note than other guests. This is not the place for brevity.

## What to Write for Different Gift Types

**Registry gifts:** Mention the specific item. "The white duvet set is already on our bed" is more meaningful than "thank you for the wonderful gift." If you genuinely do not know what you will use it for yet, at least say you are looking forward to using it.

**Cash and checks:** Do not skip mentioning the amount if it feels natural, but more important is telling them what you plan to do with it. "We're putting it toward the honeymoon" or "it's going into our house fund" gives the gift purpose and shows you thought about it. Vague gratitude for a cash gift reads hollow.

**Experience gifts:** Be specific about when you plan to use it or how excited you are about a particular aspect. "We have not booked the tasting menu reservation yet but it's already on the calendar for our one-month anniversary" works well.

**Sentimental gifts:** These deserve extra attention. A handmade quilt from your grandmother, a framed photo from your parents — spend a few extra sentences here. These are the notes people save.

**Gifts from people who did not attend:** Acknowledge their absence. "We missed having you there" or "I know you were with us in spirit" is not filler — it matters to people who wanted to be present and couldn't be.

**Group gifts from coworkers or friends:** If ten people chipped in for one gift, you can send one note addressed to all of them, but you need to name each person in the body of the note. An email to the group is also acceptable if that is how the group operates.

## The Four-Sentence Structure That Works

You do not need a new approach for every note. This structure works for almost any gift type:

1. Address the person by their preferred name, not "Dear Friend."
2. Name the specific gift.
3. Say what it means or what you will do with it — one specific sentence.
4. Add one personal line connecting to the wedding day or your relationship.
5. Close with both names.

Example for a registry gift: "Uncle Tom and Aunt Carol — thank you for the Vitamix. It arrived last week and we have already made two batches of soup. Having you at the wedding meant a lot to both of us. Love, Angel and [Partner]."

That is 38 words. It is enough. Longer is not always better.

## Handwritten vs. Printed

Handwritten notes are the standard for wedding thank yous. This is one of those etiquette positions that has held up even as everything else has shifted.

That said, if your handwriting is genuinely difficult to read, a neatly printed note with a handwritten signature and a short personal line in pen is fine. What you should avoid: fully printed, fill-in-the-blank cards with zero personal element. People notice.

If you have 150 guests and your hand cramps after 30 notes, spread the task over several days. Buy good pens that write smoothly. Put on a show you enjoy and write 20 notes per sitting.

## Addressing Envelopes Correctly

This is where couples make avoidable errors that can feel rude.

**Married couple, same last name:** "Mr. and Mrs. [Last Name]" or "[Name] and [Name] [Last Name]"

**Married couple, different last names:** Use both names: "[Name] [Last Name] and [Name] [Last Name]"

**Unmarried couple, same household:** Both names on the envelope, one gift, one note.

**Plus-one you have never met:** The invitation went to your guest only, so the envelope goes to your guest. Inside the note, you can acknowledge the plus-one by name if you know it: "We enjoyed meeting Jamie."

**A family that gave one gift:** "The [Last Name] Family" on the envelope, then list each family member's name inside.

**Children's gifts at the note:** If a child gave a gift, address the note to the child with a parent's name below it. Kids notice when they get real mail.

## What Happens If You Miss Someone

You find out three months later that you never sent a note to your college roommate. Send it now. The note you write today is better than the one you never send.

Open with a brief, non-dramatic acknowledgment: "I realize this note is long overdue." Do not spend a paragraph apologizing. Write the actual thank you and move on.

What you should not do: avoid sending the note because it feels awkward. The silence is worse than the awkward late note.
