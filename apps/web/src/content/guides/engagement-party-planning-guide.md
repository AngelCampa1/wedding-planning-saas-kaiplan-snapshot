## Who Hosts (Traditionally vs. Now)

The traditional etiquette: the bride's parents host the engagement party. In the original logic, this gave the bride's family a chance to welcome the groom's family into their social circle, which made more sense when families lived far apart and had not met.

Today it is different. Both families often live in the same city, have already met multiple times, or are spread across the country in ways that make geographic hosting decisions complicated. The "bride's parents host" rule is still acknowledged, but not strictly followed.

What actually happens more often:

- Both sets of parents co-host a single party
- The couple's friends in the wedding party throw an informal party separately
- The couple hosts themselves, especially if they are older or have established households
- No one hosts a formal engagement party at all, and announcements happen through other channels

There is no wrong answer. If the bride's parents want to host and it works logistically, let them. If no one is stepping up and you do not particularly want a formal party, skip it. If your close friends want to throw something casual, that works too.

**The one thing to avoid:** Multiple parties that split the guest list. If both sets of parents want to host separately, it creates an awkward hierarchy. Guests at one party may feel they got the lesser celebration. One event with both families participating is cleaner.

## Timing

Most engagement parties happen 1-3 months after the engagement announcement. The sweet spot is about 6-8 weeks out: enough time to plan something meaningful, not so long that the news has gone stale.

**Practical timing notes:**

- Avoid the month before your wedding. That period is for final logistics, and adding a party to the calendar is unnecessary stress.
- Avoid major holidays. A party the week before Christmas is hard to get people to, and it gets lost in other events.
- If families are geographically distant, a holiday weekend (not the actual holiday day) can make travel easier for everyone.
- Engagement parties more than 6 months after the engagement announcement feel disconnected from the news they are meant to celebrate.

## The Guest List Rule Worth Taking Seriously

Everyone at the engagement party must be invited to the wedding. This is consistent across etiquette traditions, and it exists for a reason.

When you invite someone to celebrate your engagement, you are signaling they are in your inner circle. Inviting them to that celebration and then not including them in the wedding tells them the opposite: close enough to celebrate with, but not close enough to be at the actual event.

This applies in both directions. If your engagement party list includes 60 people and you are planning a 40-person wedding, you have a problem. The people who did not make the wedding cut were just at your engagement party.

**Practical implication:** Keep the engagement party guest list smaller than your anticipated wedding guest list. If you are uncertain who will make the final wedding guest cut, do not invite them to the engagement party. It is easier to add people to a wedding guest list than to explain why someone who came to your engagement party is not invited to the wedding.

This is most commonly violated when both sets of parents are co-hosting and have different social networks with different overlap to the wedding list. Before you finalize the engagement party list, run it against your expected wedding list and reconcile any mismatches.

## Formality and Format

Engagement parties work at a range of formality levels. What matters is that the level fits the couple.

**Casual options:**
- Backyard cookout or garden party
- Dinner at a restaurant (private dining room or restaurant buyout for smaller groups)
- Home dinner party for close family and friends

**Moderate formality:**
- Cocktail hour at a rented venue or home with catered food and drinks
- Brunch at a hotel or restaurant
- Winery or brewery event

**Formal:**
- Seated dinner at a private club or restaurant
- Catered event at a family home with full service
- Venue rental with catering

The engagement party is meant to be celebratory but not as elaborate as the wedding itself. Guests at a very formal engagement party may bring more expensive gifts out of obligation, which can create gift-giving fatigue before the wedding even arrives. A warm, comfortable gathering does the job without creating expectations.

## What This Event Should and Should Not Be

**What it is:**

A celebration of the engagement, primarily for people close to the couple. An opportunity for both families to meet and spend time together if they have not. A chance for the couple to share the news with their inner circle in a personal setting.

**What it is not:**

- A second wedding. Avoid décor, a DJ, a dance floor, or anything that mimics wedding production. The wedding is the wedding.
- A gift-receiving event. No registry, no gift table, no expectation around gifts at all.
- A required stop in the wedding season. You can opt out entirely.
- A place to announce anything other than the engagement itself. Some couples use it to announce bridesmaids, share venue news, or make other wedding announcements. It is fine, but keep the focus on the engagement.

## Typical Costs

Engagement parties range widely:

**Home gathering (20-30 people):**
- Food and drinks: $300-$800 depending on what you serve
- Décor (flowers, balloons, simple centerpieces): $50-$200
- Total: $350-$1,000

**Restaurant private dining room (20-30 people):**
- Per-person food and drink: $50-$100 per guest
- Private dining room fee (some restaurants charge for this): $0-$500
- Total: $1,000-$3,500

**Catered venue event (40-60 people):**
- Venue rental: $500-$2,000
- Catering: $50-$100 per person
- Total: $2,500-$8,000

Whoever is hosting pays. If the couple is self-hosting, they pay. If the bride's parents are hosting, they pay. If guests are chipping in because they organized it, they split the cost.
