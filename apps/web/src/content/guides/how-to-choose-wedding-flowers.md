## Seasonal Availability and How It Affects Price

Most wedding guides tell you to "choose seasonal flowers." Here is what that actually means in practice.

Flowers that are available locally and in season arrive at your florist in better condition, with shorter travel time, and at lower cost because there is no long-haul import freight involved. A peony grown in the US and shipped overnight from the Pacific Northwest is fresher and cheaper than a peony imported from Colombia in October.

**US seasonal availability by flower:**

**Spring (March-June):** Peonies (May-June peak), tulips, ranunculus, anemones, sweet peas, lilac, garden roses beginning in late May, clematis, bleeding heart.

**Summer (June-September):** Garden roses at peak, sunflowers, lisianthus, lavender, zinnias, cosmos, dahlias beginning in August, gladiolus.

**Fall (September-November):** Dahlias at their absolute best, marigolds, chrysanthemums, late garden roses, dried botanicals, autumn berries, ornamental cabbages (unusual but striking).

**Winter (December-February):** Orchids (year-round), amaryllis, hellebores, winter greenery (eucalyptus, holly, pine), dried and preserved florals, forced bulb flowers in some regions.

**The cost reality of ignoring seasonality:**

A florist can get you peonies in October. They come from South America. The price per stem jumps from $3-$5 (peak domestic season) to $7-$12. The quality is often lower: more petal browning, faster decay. For 20 bridal party bouquets and ceremony arrangements using peonies, this price difference can add $500-$1,000 to your floral total.

If you have a specific flower that is central to your vision and it is out of season, budget for it intentionally rather than being surprised. And ask your florist for in-season alternatives that achieve a similar look. Ranunculus, for instance, has a similar layered, lush quality to peonies at a fraction of the out-of-season peony price.

## How to Communicate Your Aesthetic to a Florist

The phrase "I'll know it when I see it" is the most expensive sentence in wedding planning.

Florists need specific direction to give you an accurate quote. The more specific your input, the more accurate their estimate. Vague input produces vague quotes that may not reflect what you actually want, and then costs change when you clarify later.

**What to bring to your first florist consultation:**

- A folder of 8-10 reference images (Pinterest, Instagram, real wedding photos). These should be consistent with each other. Do not bring 5 romantic garden-style images and 5 modern minimal images.
- Your color palette with specific names or hex codes, or fabric swatches if you have them.
- The names of any specific flowers you want or want to avoid.
- A list of what arrangements you need: bridal bouquet, bridesmaid bouquets (how many), boutonnieres (how many), ceremony arbor or chuppah, number of reception tables and approximate centerpiece size.
- Your total floral budget.

**Describing style:**

Instead of "romantic," say: "loose, garden-style, overflowing slightly at the edges, not tight and structured."
Instead of "simple," say: "minimal stems, lots of negative space, three flowers max per arrangement, no greenery filler."
Instead of "colorful," say: "jewel tones: burgundy, deep plum, rust, with minimal greenery."

**Tell your florist what you do not want.** If you hate baby's breath, say so. If you find tropical flowers (birds of paradise, anthuriums) jarring, say so. If you love texture but hate flat, single-variety arrangements, say so.

## Photography vs. In-Person Appearance

Flowers look different in person than they do in photographs. Understanding this changes which flowers are worth your budget.

**Flowers that photograph beautifully:**

- Garden roses. Multiple layers of petals, clear texture, holds detail in photos well.
- Ranunculus. Similar layered structure, photographs almost as well as garden roses at lower cost.
- Dahlias. Incredible photographic texture, especially cafe au lait and dinner plate varieties.
- Protea. Unusual, striking, holds detail.
- Peonies. Lush and photographically rich, though they blow open fast in heat.

**Flowers that can lose impact in photographs:**

- White flowers in general. In bright light or flash photography, white petals blow out and lose their detail. If you want white flowers, ask your photographer how they handle white blooms.
- Flat, single-layer flowers (gerbera daisies, carnations). Less photographic dimension.
- Greenery-heavy arrangements. Greenery can recede in photos, making an arrangement look sparser than it does in person.

**Flowers that look stunning in person but do not photograph as interestingly:**

- Dusty miller (silver-gray foliage). Gorgeous tactile texture; goes flat in photos.
- Sweet peas. Delicate, beautiful, almost disappears photographically.

Ask your photographer and florist to consult together on this if it matters to you. A florist experienced with photographed events has already solved this problem.

## Stem Count vs. Arrangement Volume

This is where you can manage cost without reducing visual impact.

**Ways to create visual volume economically:**

**Greenery as a structural element.** A centerpiece built on a base of eucalyptus, ferns, or tropical leaves can look abundant before a single premium bloom is added. Greenery is cheap; its contribution to volume is significant.

**Large blooms over many small blooms.** Three dinner plate dahlias make more visual impact than fifteen spray roses at similar or lower cost. Big blooms look expensive.

**Height and structure.** A tall, dramatic arrangement requires fewer flowers than a wide, sprawling one. Branches, tall grasses, and structural foliage (monstera, banana leaf) add height without adding flower cost.

**Vase choice affects perceived value.** A large loose arrangement in a $5 bud vase looks less intentional than the same flowers in a proper vessel. Vase rentals through your florist or an event rental company ($5-$25 per vessel) affect the final look significantly.

## DIY Reality Check

DIY wedding flowers work in specific circumstances. Here is an honest assessment:

**When DIY makes sense:**

- Your wedding has under 50 guests and under 10 arrangements total
- Someone in your group has actual flower arrangement experience
- You have access to a refrigerator large enough to store arrangements overnight
- You have a workspace (a garage, a large table) to assemble arrangements the day before
- You have done a test run with your actual flowers

**When DIY does not make sense:**

- You have more than 15-20 arrangements to make
- Your wedding is on a Saturday (flowers arrive Friday at the earliest, you assemble Friday night after work while exhausted)
- You are managing other wedding-day-eve logistics
- You have never actually arranged flowers for a real event

**If you do DIY:** Order 20-30% more stems than you think you need. Budget for stems going unused. Flowers are fragile and some will not be usable. Order from a wholesale supplier like FiftyFlowers or Mayesh rather than grocery store flower buckets for cost and quality.

## Where to Cut When Budget Gets Tight

If your floral quote comes back over budget, these cuts have the most impact with the least sacrifice:

**1. Reduce centerpiece count, not centerpiece quality.** If you have 20 tables, consider alternating tall arrangements on 10 tables and lower, candle-heavy arrangements on 10 tables. You are now making 10 expensive arrangements instead of 20.

**2. Repurpose ceremony flowers at the reception.** Ceremony arch arrangements, aisle markers, and pew arrangements can be moved to the cocktail hour or reception after the ceremony. Work with your florist on how to design them for dual use.

**3. Cut personal flowers for non-wedding-party members.** Corsages for mothers and grandmothers are traditional but not essential. If it is a choice between corsages and a better centerpiece design, most couples and their families are fine skipping the corsages.

**4. Use non-floral elements as volume.** Candles, lanterns, books, fruit, and foliage add table presence without adding floral cost.

**5. Ask your florist directly: "What would you cut from this quote to bring it to $X without compromising the overall look?"** A good florist has dealt with this question hundreds of times and knows exactly which items to pull.
