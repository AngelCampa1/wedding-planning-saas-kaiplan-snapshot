## The Problem With "Feature-Rich" Apps

Wedding app marketing follows a predictable pattern. You land on the homepage and see a list: budget tracker, vendor directory, guest list, seating chart, wedding website, photo sharing, AI recommendations, inspiration boards, collaborative planning, checklist, timeline, and more.

That list looks reassuring. It feels like you'd be covered.

The honest version: most couples actively use 4-5 features. The rest sits unused, occasionally causes confusion, and exists primarily because it photographs well in app store screenshots and marketing decks.

This guide is a direct answer to the question: what will you actually use?

## Features You'll Use Every Week

### Real Budget Tracking With Line Items

This is the non-negotiable. The version that's actually useful has all of the following:

- A line item for each vendor with the contracted amount
- A field for each payment made, with date and amount
- A running balance showing what's still owed
- A payment due date for the next installment
- A total view showing all committed spend against your overall budget

This is a ledger. Not an estimate calculator, not a pie chart of recommended spending percentages, not a "how does your budget compare to the national average" graphic.

When you have 10 signed contracts with varying payment schedules running simultaneously, the only way to stay on top of it is a real ledger. Couples who use estimate calculators and call it budgeting are the ones who end up $8,000 over with three months to go.

The test: open the budget section of any tool and ask yourself, "Can I record that I paid a $1,500 deposit to my photographer on March 15, and see that I have a $3,200 balance remaining, due August 1?" If the answer is no, it's not a budget tracker.

### Vendor Payment Scheduling

Related to budget tracking, but worth calling out separately because it's the specific gap that causes the most stress.

A payment schedule for a typical wedding looks something like this:

- Venue: 20% deposit at booking, 50% at 6 months out, 30% final payment 30 days before
- Caterer: $500 deposit, 50% at 3 months, final headcount and balance 2 weeks before
- Photographer: $1,000 deposit at booking, balance 1 month before
- Band/DJ: 50% deposit, 50% day-of or night before
- Florist: 50% deposit, 50% balance 2 weeks before

That's 12-15 payment events spread over 12-18 months. Missing one triggers late fees at best, contract complications at worst. A planning app that shows you all upcoming payment due dates in a single view is worth its cost just for this.

Without it, you're managing payment schedules across your email inbox and your own memory. That's how things get missed.

### Guest List With Meal Tracking

The minimum viable guest list feature:

- Add guests with names and contact info
- Track RSVP status: not yet sent, invited, attending, declined
- Meal preference or selection (chicken, fish, vegetarian, kids meal)
- Dietary restrictions
- Export to CSV for sharing with your caterer

Why meal tracking specifically? Because you'll need to give your caterer a final headcount and meal selection breakdown 2-4 weeks before the wedding. If your planning app stores this information, that process is a CSV export. If it doesn't, you're manually compiling it from RSVP cards, email replies, and your own notes.

Bonus features that are genuinely useful: table assignment connected to the guest list, plus/minus tracking (who's bringing a date), and address collection for invitation mailing.

### A Checklist With Actual Dates

Not a generic checklist that says "book photographer (12 months out)." A checklist that starts from your actual wedding date and tells you that based on that date, you should be booking your photographer by a specific date, confirming your menu by another specific date, and sending invitations by a third specific date.

The generic timeline checklist is fine as a starting point. Its limitation is that it doesn't adapt to your situation. If your wedding is 8 months away, you're already behind on some "12 months out" tasks and need a different plan than someone who's 14 months out.

The best planning checklists are date-anchored and update automatically when your wedding date changes. The minimum acceptable bar is a customizable checklist where you can set your own due dates.

## Features You'll Use Occasionally

### Seating Chart

If you're doing assigned seating (most formal and semi-formal weddings do), a seating chart tool is genuinely useful. The ability to drag guests between tables, see headcounts per table, and visualize the full room is better than doing it with a spreadsheet.

That said, seating happens once, usually in the last 4-6 weeks before the wedding. It's important during that sprint, then done. This is a feature worth having but not one that should drive your tool choice.

### Vendor Contact Storage

A place to store your vendors' names, phone numbers, emails, and notes. This is less sophisticated than it sounds, but it's genuinely useful as a single source of truth for "what's the name of our florist's assistant who handles day-of coordination?"

The basic version is a contact card per vendor. Better versions add notes fields, booking status, and contract details. This is something you'll reference regularly but not update frequently.

### Day-of Timeline or Run-of-Show

Some apps let you build a minute-by-minute day-of timeline: ceremony starts at 4pm, cocktail hour starts at 5pm, first dance at 7:15pm, cake cutting at 8pm. This is useful for sharing with your coordinator, venue, and vendors.

Most couples build this document anyway for their vendors. Having it inside the planning app is convenient but not essential. A Google Doc shared with your vendors accomplishes the same thing.

## Features That Look Good but You Won't Use

### Inspiration Boards and Mood Boards

Pinterest exists. Pinterest is better at this than any wedding app. If you want to save photos of floral arrangements, dress styles, table settings, and venue aesthetics, you'll do it on Pinterest, Instagram, or a saved folder on your phone.

The mood board feature in planning apps gets created, gets three photos added to it, and then never gets opened again. This is a feature for marketing screenshots, not for actual planning behavior.

### AI Vendor Recommendations

The promise: AI suggests vendors in your area based on your style, budget, and preferences. The reality: AI recommendations in wedding apps tend to surface the same popular, highly-reviewed vendors everyone else books. The vendors with quirky, specific, or niche styles that might be perfect for you are the ones AI recommendations miss.

Better vendor discovery: ask your venue who they work with regularly. Ask recently married couples in local Facebook groups. Search Instagram for vendors who do the specific style you want. All three of these outperform app-generated recommendations.

Some tools that earn from vendor advertising describe their recommendations as "AI-powered" when they're actually "pay-to-play-powered." Knowing how the tool earns revenue tells you more about recommendation quality than the AI marketing language does.

### Social Feed and Community Features

Some apps have a social component where couples share their planning progress, ask questions, or see what others are doing. We've never heard a couple describe this as a feature they actually used. For community, couples use Reddit (r/weddingplanning is genuinely useful), local Facebook groups, and The Knot's forums.

A social feed in a planning app is adding complexity to a tool that should be helping you manage logistics. It's a retention feature for the app, not a productivity feature for you.

### Wedding Magazine and Editorial Content

"This season's top venue trends" or "35 floral arrangements you need to see" articles are content marketing, not planning tools. The apps that include editorial content as a core feature are apps whose business model depends on keeping you engaged and clicking toward vendor bookings.

There's nothing wrong with reading about wedding trends. But if your planning app includes a built-in magazine section, recognize that this content exists to serve the app's vendor relationships, not your planning process.

### Registry Integration Within the Planning App

This one is more nuanced. Registry and planning are genuinely related activities. But unless the app's registry feature is as good as Zola's or Amazon's dedicated registries, you're likely to end up with a second-rate registry experience inside your planning tool.

Zola has spent years building a great registry product. Using Zola for registry and a separate tool for planning is often a better outcome than trying to use a planning app that does both adequately.

## The Feature Checklist Before You Commit

Before choosing a planning app, confirm it can do these specific things:

**Budget tracking:**
- [ ] Record actual contract amount per vendor
- [ ] Record partial payments (deposits) with dates
- [ ] Show remaining balance per vendor
- [ ] Show next payment due date per vendor
- [ ] Show total committed spend vs total budget

**Vendor management:**
- [ ] Store vendor contact information
- [ ] Track booking status (considering, contacted, booked)
- [ ] Record contract notes or key terms

**Guest list:**
- [ ] Add guests and track RSVP status
- [ ] Record meal preferences per guest
- [ ] Export guest list as CSV for caterer use

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Checklist tied to your actual wedding date
- [ ] Customizable, not just a template you can't modify

If an app checks all of these boxes, it's worth serious consideration. If it's missing more than one item in budget tracking, pass on it for a tool that takes financial tracking seriously.
