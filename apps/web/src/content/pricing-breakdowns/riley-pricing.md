## Who Riley Prices Its Product For

Riley is sold to professional wedding planners, not to couples. The pricing reflects that: starting at ~$12/month for a basic plan and scaling for professional-level feature sets, it's business software for someone managing 15-30 client weddings per year. Those economics don't apply to a couple managing one wedding.

That doesn't make Riley's pricing unfair — it makes it irrelevant to couples evaluating their own planning tool options. The price a professional planner pays for Riley, and how that cost gets embedded into their service fee, is a different question from what a couple should budget for their planning app.

## The Real Cost Calculation for Couples

For couples considering whether to hire a planner who uses Riley, the relevant cost calculation is the planner's service fee, not Riley's subscription.

Full-service wedding planners charge 10-15% of the total wedding budget. For a $40,000 wedding, that's $4,000-$6,000. Day-of coordinators are $800-$2,000. Partial planning packages fall in between.

Riley's $12-40/month subscription is the planner's business overhead. It will show up in the planner's rate, but as a small fraction of a much larger service fee.

The decision couples should make first is whether professional planning services are worth the cost at all. If yes, evaluate planners on their experience and fit, not which software they use.

## For Self-Planning Couples

If you've decided to plan your own wedding without hiring a professional, you don't need Riley and can't purchase it as an individual couple.

You need a direct-to-couple planning tool. The options include:

Free, ad-supported tools like The Knot and Zola — both have planning features built around their core business models (vendor advertising and registry commerce, respectively).

Paid planning tools like Kaiplan — $20/month or $100 one-time lifetime access. Kaiplan is built for couples managing their own planning: real budget ledger, vendor management with payment tracking, guest list, and seating chart.

The difference between free and paid tools is primarily the depth of the planning features and the revenue model behind them. Free tools earn from vendors or registries; paid tools earn from couple subscriptions. Both models have trade-offs worth understanding before committing to a platform for 18 months.
